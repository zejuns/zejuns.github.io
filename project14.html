A Ballad of Tenacious Life

In Memory of My Grandfather, Huang Mizheng (1944–2024)

“Not to glorify suffering, but to remember it — a quiet monument to love and endurance.”
— Written in 2019; in memory of my grandfather, who passed away in 2024.

My grandfather’s name was Huang Mizheng. He was born in 1944 in Anji, Zhejiang Province. He was born in the old society and grew up in the new one. At the tender age of six or seven, he witnessed the land reforms—land redistribution, marking the end of the landlord economy. As I trace the arc of his life, I am struck by the ruggedness of his journey and the resilience he showed in the face of overwhelming adversity. His was a life that bore witness to the quiet strength of the human spirit.

My grandfather was one of twelve children—six sons, four daughters, and a pair of twins who died before birth. I looked at the old photographs pressed under glass, their yellowed edges whispering of time gone by. The faces in those photos felt distant—some forever beyond my reach, like shadows from a time long passed.

I sat beside the brazier, flames licking the quiet air, glowing with hues of red and orange, casting a warm light across the window. It was New Year’s Eve, and Grandpa began to recount his stories of the past…

I. Origins


Our ancestral home was not in Anji, but in a small county called Huangyan in Zhejiang Province. Back then, we still had some farmland, but with too many children and repeated crop failures, life grew increasingly difficult—so much so that we could only afford two meals a day. In order to raise two children, my great-great-grandfather was forced to sell what little land we had to the landlord in exchange for grain.

As land diminished, so too did hope. At the worst times, the porridge thinned to mere rice water. Wild greens became staple food. Their home was empty save for two beds, a cabinet, and a stove. With no other options, my great-great-grandfather had to seek a new way forward—pioneering new land. He carried a pole across his shoulders: in one basket, the two children; in the other, all their remaining belongings. And so they set off.

They begged as they walked, searching for shelter. It’s unimaginable to me. If I were to travel today without a cent, I wouldn’t even know how to beg, let alone survive. But that was the old agrarian society—a world where people’s lives were inextricably tied to the land, and survival without it was unimaginable.

II. By the River


After enduring countless hardships, they finally reached the banks of the Qiantang River. My great-great-grandfather carried a heavy load on his shoulders, with beads of sweat the size of soybeans slowly rolling down his cheeks. He was worrying about the road ahead—afraid he wouldn’t be able to raise the two children, and even considered throwing the younger one (my great-grandfather) into the river.

At this point, Grandpa suddenly turned to me and asked a question that left me utterly surprised: “Do you know what Guanyin soil is?”

In those desperate times of famine, when all food had been exhausted and nature offered no more relief, people turned to whatever they could find for survival. In some places, a pale, powdery soil—later called “Guanyin soil”—was used to make makeshift steamed buns to stave off hunger.

Though it looked like flour and could suppress hunger for a short time, this soil was not digestible. It provided no nutrition and could lead to severe discomfort. Yet for many, it represented the last glimmer of hope in the face of starvation—a silent witness to that harsh era.

Grandpa continued his story. Just as my great-great-grandfather was about to throw the child into the Qiantang River, his wife stopped him. Yes—they were only afraid the children would grow up to suffer as they had. But as long as the family could stay together, even if they had to survive on Guanyin soil, they would face whatever lay ahead, undaunted.

They crossed mountains and rivers, fearless and unwavering, and in the end, they arrived in Anji—alive.

III. Roots in a New Land


You might not know the plant Aspidistra elatior, but it provides the leaves we use to wrap zongzi—traditional Chinese rice dumplings. When my ancestors first arrived in Anji, life was still incredibly hard. They survived by eating the fruit of this hardy plant.

They ate wild herbs and fruits, borrowed tools, and began cultivating rice. Through diligence and determination, they finally established themselves in this new land.

Grandpa paused, looked at the fire—which had dimmed—and got up to add more wood. Night had deepened, and the stars outside glimmered brighter than ever.

My great-grandfather was a true farmer. He worked hard through two harvests each year to support his family, without rest even during the height of summer.

IV. Reform and Resistance


My grandfather was the third child in the family. Though still a child, he had to act as an adult and care for his siblings. On clear days, he worked the fields; on rainy ones, he wove straw sandals—there was no money for shoes. During the New Year, the family was allotted only six feet of cloth coupons, sometimes ten at most—barely enough for a set of clothes.

Grandpa started herding cattle at seven and entered school late, as was common in rural areas. On a school trip to Baofu Town, he saw electric light for the first time—his first real glimpse of science. In contrast, by 1906 electric bulbs were already widely used in the West.

Not long after, the village built a small hydroelectric station. Though not every household had electricity, the old kerosene lamps began to disappear.

Grandpa completed six years of elementary school. At eighteen, during the Three Years of Natural Disasters, food ran out. He lived on just two taels (about 75 grams) of rice per meal. His hands trembled when he wrote; his face yellowed from malnutrition, and he became a shadow of himself.

Eventually, like most of his peers, he dropped out. The spark of knowledge—a brief meteor streaking across the sky—stirred great waves in his young heart, only to fade into silence.

V. Labor and Legacy


The Huangpu River is the landmark waterway of Shanghai. Few know that its source lies in the Shikeng Reservoir. Construction began in 1958, when Grandpa was still a teenager. He helped build it—hauling mud every day for seven “work points.” His labor wasn’t just for points—it was for the future, to relieve droughts and irrigate fields.

That same year, the government launched the Great Leap Forward, a campaign to build socialism “faster, better, and more economically.” In our town of Baofu, they built a large steel furnace and over thirty small ones. Grandpa’s life changed again. That period of famine remains a heavy chapter in China’s history. Many people fell ill from hunger, and Grandpa endured one of the hardest times of his life.

Later, Grandpa started his own family and continued working on the collective farm—until today…

His story ended. The sky began to pale, and weary stars shut their tired eyes. A new year had arrived. As I laid down my brush, Grandpa had just turned 75.

Time flies like a white horse leaping across a crack in the sky. A single life is long and full of stories—but all I have is my humble pen to capture fleeting fragments. Fewer than 3,000 words—how could that ever contain an entire life?

Postscript


Looking back at my grandfather’s life, every step was a struggle, each footprint pressed deep into the soil. To live was to feel life’s fragility before nature’s might. Yet survival itself revealed an inner strength that refuses to bend.

This resilience did not belong to my grandfather alone—it was shared by countless others who, even amidst famine and war, held on to hope and refused to yield. In their hearts, it forged a spirit of fearlessness and an unshakable will to keep moving forward.

Speaking of ancestors, I think of generation names—a millennia-old Chinese naming tradition, a form of ritual, of culture. I used to think it was long outdated, until I learned that my grandfather bore the generational name “Mi,” and my great-grandfather “Xian.”

Generation names link families across time. They are the thread that binds us. Such customs are fading, vanishing into the currents of history. And so are lives—lives that won’t be recorded in textbooks. Not everyone becomes a Lu Xun or a Qian Xuesen. But that doesn’t mean they should be forgotten.

History will remember them—if we choose to write it down. Just as I’ve done here.


I hope to internalize this resilience and let it inspire me — to face any hardship head-on, without avoidance.
